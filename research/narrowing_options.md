# Narrowing the essay after the proposal feedback

The professor awarded the proposal an A and wrote: “This is a great proposal. It may be a little broad.” This supports retaining the government question while choosing one principal case and one evaluative claim. It is not evidence that the professor endorsed the draft's particular empirical conclusions.

## 1. Recommended: What counts as Congress protecting NSF?

**Research question:** How should Congress's protection of NSF in FY2026 be evaluated against the presidential request, historical purchasing power, and its own authorized goals?

**Proposed thesis:** Congress used its power of the purse to prevent most of the president's proposed FY2026 reduction, yet NSF's decade-long funding record shows that resisting a cut can coexist with declining purchasing power and an unfulfilled legislative expansion plan. Congressional protection should therefore be assessed against what appropriations can support and the goals Congress authorized, as well as against the president's request.

This is one institutional argument, with NSF as its case. FY2017–FY2026 establishes context; FY2023–FY2026 supplies the central authorization–appropriation conflict. It retains the proposal's question about congressional protection but makes the measurable outcome explicit.

**Evidence already available:** NSF appropriations grew 17.1% nominally from FY2017 to FY2026, while falling 11.5% in GDP-adjusted terms (FY2026 deflator estimated). The decline through FY2025 is 5.7%, or 8.1% after removing unavailable emergency funding. In FY2026 Congress offset 94.0% of the proposed reduction against the FY2025 enacted baseline, yet provided 49.1% of that year's CHIPS authorization. These are different, complementary benchmarks: bargaining outcome, purchasing power and legislative ambition.

**Strongest counterargument:** An authorization is a ceiling/permission or stated target, not guaranteed spending. Congress can legitimately revise its priorities when appropriating, and a national GDP deflator does not measure every scientific input. Answer by defending transparent, explicit choices about real resources and authorized goals—not by treating the authorization gap as missing cash or proof of illegality.

**What to keep:** Section 2 as the core; the appropriation/request distinction; a short personal explanation of why scientific planning matters; the course's power-of-the-purse framework; peer-reviewed funding-interruption evidence as a limited explanation of why stability matters.

**What to reduce:** Bush/Kilgore history to a few sentences; NASA and Roman to one comparison or concluding application; detailed FY2027 division cuts; NIH patenting; the separate multiyear/bridge-funding/reporting/review policy menu. End with one proposal: congressional budget justifications should compare real resources with authorized objectives and identify the research commitments affected by a lower appropriation. Retain implementation oversight as a boundary on what budget totals alone can establish.

**Possible title:** “Saving NSF from Cuts: What Congress's FY2026 Budget Actually Protected.”

**Argument sequence:** Define protection → show the ten-year nominal/real difference → examine FY2026 request and enacted outcome → compare with CHIPS → answer fiscal-flexibility objections → defend the more demanding benchmark for congressional accountability.

## 2. Strong alternative: NASA's choices between Science and Exploration

**Research question:** How has federal budget policy changed the relative priority of NASA Science and Exploration since FY2017?

**Proposed thesis:** The decade-long divergence between NASA Science and Exploration shows why agency-wide budget totals are an inadequate test of support for space research: Congress and presidents make consequential choices within NASA, and those choices should be defended in terms of the scientific work they support or displace.

**Evidence:** From FY2017 to FY2026, Science changes by −4.9% and Exploration by +36.0% in GDP-adjusted terms, with an estimated FY2026 deflator. Through FY2025 the corresponding changes are −1.0% and +37.9%. The FY2027 request would widen the difference, but remains a proposal in this analysis. The pattern establishes relative funding priority; it does not establish a dollar-for-dollar transfer, the motivations of individual lawmakers, or that all Exploration spending lacks scientific value.

**What this would require:** Focus the essay on NASA; examine the legislation and program objectives behind the divergence; assess the strongest human-exploration rationale. Preserve the BPS accounting caveat. Do not use the unadjusted Astrophysics row across the Webb reclassification, or infer Roman grant cuts from a total Science budget.

**Tradeoff:** Closest to the author's astrophysics interests and visually strong. It changes the proposal's principal case from NSF to NASA and needs more program-level evidence before asserting why particular choices occurred.

**Possible title:** “Which Space Program? Science and Exploration in NASA's Federal Budget.”

## 3. Closest to the current prose: Protecting an NSF award after appropriation

**Research question:** What oversight is needed to make Congress's appropriation effective when NSF terminates previously approved awards?

**Proposed thesis:** The 2025 NSF terminations expose a gap between Congress providing agency resources and securing continuity for approved research; award-level reporting and reasoned review are necessary to make funding decisions accountable to Congress.

**Evidence already present:** NSF terminations reported by GAO, changes in agency priorities, reinstatements, and peer-reviewed evidence on the consequences of NIH renewal delays. The ten-year budget plots become context, not the main test.

**What this would require:** A focused analysis of termination policies, reinstatements, agency authority and congressional reporting tools. A dated, award-level NSF dataset would be necessary if we also wanted to quantify net terminations, field-specific losses or employment effects; it is not a prerequisite for an institutional argument. Aggregate budgets and NIH employment studies cannot establish how many NSF physics jobs or projects were lost. Distinguish deliberate terminations from administrative renewal delays when proposing remedies.

**Tradeoff:** Strong government mechanism and closest to the draft's current thesis, but the budget plots would play a smaller role. Quantifying effects would require a different data project. NIH results help motivate a question; they do not measure the effect of NSF terminations.

**Possible title:** “After Appropriation: Congressional Oversight of NSF Grant Terminations.”
